# Data Modelling Overview

Data modelling is the practice of deciding what data means, how it is
represented, how entities relate, and how the structure supports its intended
users. A good model makes valid questions easy to answer and invalid questions
hard to ask accidentally.

The central rule is simple:

> Define the grain before writing the joins.

## 1. Why data modelling matters

Without an explicit model, a dataset can look plausible while producing wrong
counts, duplicated measures, inconsistent filters, and ambiguous definitions.
Modelling provides a shared contract for:

- the meaning of each row and column;
- the identity of entities;
- the relationships between entities;
- the level at which measures are calculated;
- the history that should be retained;
- the consumers and their expected questions.

The model is not only a diagram. It is represented by tables, keys,
constraints, transformations, tests, documentation, and semantic relationships
in the BI layer.

## 2. Levels of modelling

### Conceptual model

The business view: entities and relationships without implementation detail.

```text
Company publishes Job Posting.
Job Posting requires Skill.
Job Posting is located in Location.
```

### Logical model

The detailed structure: attributes, keys, cardinality, optionality, and
business rules. It is still mostly independent of a specific database engine.

### Physical model

The implementation: tables, columns, data types, indexes, partitions,
constraints, schemas, and materializations in a particular database.

## 3. Entities, attributes, and relationships

An entity is a thing the business recognizes, such as a company, customer,
job, order, product, or skill. An attribute describes an entity. A relationship
connects entities.

State cardinality explicitly:

```text
one company -> many job postings
one job posting -> many required skills
one skill -> many job postings
```

The last relationship is many-to-many. It needs a relationship table or bridge:

```text
job_postings
    |
    +-- bridge_job_posting_skills -- skills
```

## 4. Grain

Grain is the precise meaning of one row in a table or model.

Examples:

```text
One row per company.
One row per active job posting.
One row per job posting and skill.
One row per company and calendar month.
```

Grain is not the same as the primary key, although a key should identify one
row at that grain. A model can have a surrogate key while its business grain is
still `job_posting_id + skill_id`.

### 4.1 Why grain prevents bugs

Suppose a job has three skills and a compensation table has two rows. Joining
both child tables directly creates six combinations. A salary total may become
three times too large.

The safe choices are:

- keep each child relationship at its own grain;
- aggregate each child to the job grain before joining;
- keep the relationship as a bridge and let the consumer navigate it.

Never add `DISTINCT` as a first response to duplicated measures. Identify the
grain-changing join.

### 4.2 Grain statement template

Every model description should be able to complete this sentence:

```text
One row represents __________________________________.
The row is uniquely identified by ____________________.
```

Also document whether the population includes inactive, deleted, test, or
historical records.

## 5. Keys and identity

### Natural keys

A natural key comes from the business or source system, such as an external job
ID or ISO country code. It is meaningful but can change or collide across
sources.

### Surrogate keys

A surrogate key is generated by the database or transformation layer. It is
stable inside the model and useful for warehouse relationships, especially for
historical dimension rows.

### Composite keys

A composite key uses several columns together, such as:

```text
job_posting_id + skill_id
```

Use a composite uniqueness test even if a separate technical ID exists. The
technical ID does not protect the business grain by itself.

### Cross-source identity

Two source records may represent the same business entity. Keep the original
source identity and add a canonical identity only when the matching rule is
explicit, tested, and explainable. Never silently overwrite source IDs.

## 6. Normalization for operational systems

Normalization reduces duplicated facts and update anomalies in operational
databases.

### First normal form

Columns contain atomic values rather than repeating groups or comma-separated
lists. A job's skills should not normally be stored as one text field if skills
need to be filtered, counted, or related to a skill dictionary.

### Second normal form

Non-key attributes depend on the entire key. This matters mainly for tables
with composite keys.

### Third normal form

Non-key attributes depend on the key and not on another non-key attribute. For
example, a department name belongs in a department table rather than repeated
in every employee row if the department is an entity with its own identity.

Normalization is useful for write correctness and maintenance. It is not a
requirement that every BI query join dozens of operational tables.

## 7. Dimensional modelling for analytics

Dimensional modelling organizes analytical data around facts and dimensions.
The common shape is a star schema:

```text
                 dim_company
                      |
dim_date ---- fct_job_postings ---- dim_location
                      |
                 dim_job
                      |
          bridge_job_posting_skills
                      |
                 dim_skill
```

The goal is predictable filtering, understandable measures, and a stable
interface for BI tools.

## 8. Fact tables

A fact table records events, measurements, or states at a declared grain.

Examples:

- one row per order;
- one row per job posting;
- one row per posting and skill requirement;
- one row per account and day balance;
- one row per monthly company snapshot.

Facts usually contain foreign keys to dimensions, dates, degenerate business
identifiers, and numeric measures or flags.

### 8.1 Types of fact tables

| Type | Example | Characteristic |
|---|---|---|
| Transaction fact | One order line | One row per event |
| Periodic snapshot | Daily account balance | One row per entity and period |
| Accumulating snapshot | Recruitment pipeline | Row progresses through milestones |
| Factless fact | Attendance or eligibility | Relationship/event without numeric measure |

### 8.2 Additivity

Measures behave differently across dimensions:

- additive: revenue can be summed across products and time;
- semi-additive: account balance can be summed across accounts but not across
  time;
- non-additive: a ratio or percentage should usually be recalculated from its
  components, not summed.

Document how each measure can be aggregated.

## 9. Dimension tables

Dimensions provide descriptive context for facts. A dimension can contain
attributes used for filtering, grouping, labeling, and drill-down.

Examples:

- `dim_company`: company name, industry, size;
- `dim_skill`: canonical skill name and skill family;
- `dim_location`: city, region, country;
- `dim_date`: day, week, month, quarter, year;
- `dim_job`: title, seniority, employment type.

Dimensions should have a stable key and clear handling for unknown or missing
members. A conventional `unknown` dimension row can preserve a fact when its
descriptive match is not yet available.

### 9.1 Slowly changing dimensions

Slowly changing dimensions (SCD) define what happens when attributes change.

| Type | Behavior | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Type 0 | Never update the original value | Original classification must remain fixed |
| Type 1 | Overwrite the old value | History is not needed |
| Type 2 | Add a new versioned row | Historical analysis must preserve prior context |
| Type 3 | Keep limited previous values | A small amount of history is enough |

Type 2 rows commonly have a surrogate key, effective start/end timestamps, an
`is_current` flag, and a natural business key.

## 10. Bridges and many-to-many relationships

A bridge represents a many-to-many relationship without duplicating the parent
fact.

```text
bridge_job_posting_skills
-------------------------
job_posting_key
skill_key
requirement_type
proficiency_level
priority
```

The bridge grain is one row per job posting and skill, optionally including
attributes of that relationship. Its uniqueness rule is normally the composite
business key plus any versioning columns.

Use a bridge when the relationship itself matters or when a job can have many
skills. Do not flatten all skills into repeated columns such as `skill_1`,
`skill_2`, and `skill_3`.

## 11. What belongs in Power BI versus the data model

The database or dbt layer should usually prepare:

- stable keys and relationships;
- cleaned and typed values;
- canonical identities;
- reusable business classifications;
- facts, dimensions, and bridges at explicit grains;
- row-level eligibility rules that define the official dataset.

Power BI should usually provide:

- relationships between published tables;
- user-selected filters and slicers;
- presentation measures and formatting;
- interactive calculations that are intentionally report-specific.

Do not physically filter a fact table by every possible dimension selection in
advance. A fact should normally contain keys to dimensions, and the BI model
should propagate filters through relationships.

There are two important exceptions:

1. a business rule defines the mart population, such as an official
   `active_jobs` dataset;
2. a pre-aggregated or denormalized table is deliberately created for a known
   performance or usability need.

For a job market model, a good default is:

```text
fct_job_postings          one row per selected job posting
bridge_job_posting_skills one row per job posting and skill
dim_skill                 one row per canonical skill
```

Power BI can filter `fct_job_postings` by `dim_skill` through the bridge when
the relationship directions and many-to-many behavior are configured safely.
If a visual needs one row per job and a list of skills, create an additional
job-grain aggregate with a concatenated display field; do not replace the
normalized bridge.

## 12. Layers: staging, intermediate, and marts

### Staging

Staging is close to the source. It standardizes names and types, exposes source
data consistently, and applies simple source-level cleanup.

```text
source table -> stg_source_table
```

### Intermediate

Intermediate models combine and prepare concepts. They are reusable building
blocks, not necessarily direct BI outputs.

```text
staging -> int_canonical_jobs -> int_job_skill_requirements
```

### Marts

Marts are consumer-facing facts, dimensions, bridges, and aggregates. Their
grain, business definitions, and tests form an explicit data contract.

```text
intermediate -> fct_job_postings
intermediate -> dim_skills
intermediate -> bridge_job_posting_skills
```

Avoid having every mart read operational sources directly. A staging boundary
makes source changes visible and keeps source cleanup consistent. A direct read
can be acceptable for a genuinely simple one-off model, but it should be a
conscious exception.

## 13. Choosing a model grain: practical process

Use this sequence for a new analytical requirement:

1. Write the business question in plain language.
2. List the entities, events, and measures involved.
3. State the required output grain in one sentence.
4. Identify the source keys and whether they are stable.
5. Map each measure to the grain where it is naturally true.
6. Identify one-to-many and many-to-many joins.
7. Decide which relationships remain separate bridges.
8. Define active, historical, deleted, and unknown populations.
9. Choose model layers and materializations.
10. Add uniqueness, relationship, null, and business-rule tests.
11. Validate row counts and totals against a trusted source.
12. Document the contract for BI consumers.

## 14. Time and history

Decide whether a dataset represents:

- the current state only;
- events as they occurred;
- periodic snapshots;
- historical versions of descriptive attributes.

These are different grains. A current job-posting table cannot answer how the
posting looked six months ago unless history or snapshots are retained.

Use half-open time ranges for events:

```text
start <= event_time < end
```

Define the timezone and the meaning of dates such as `published_date`,
`retrieved_date`, and `closed_date`. A date in a source may be a local calendar
date, not an instant.

## 15. Data quality rules

Good models make assumptions testable:

| Assumption | Example check |
|---|---|
| Key exists | Not-null test |
| Key identifies one row | Uniqueness test |
| Parent exists | Relationship test |
| Value is controlled | Accepted-values test |
| Grain is preserved | Composite uniqueness test |
| Source is recent enough | Freshness check |
| Measure is plausible | Range or reconciliation test |
| Business rule holds | Singular SQL test |

Prefer failing loudly over silently producing a plausible but incorrect metric.
Warnings are appropriate for known transitional conditions; key integrity and
measure correctness usually deserve errors.

## 16. Performance and usability

Modelling choices affect both database performance and BI usability.

Consider:

- row counts at each layer;
- selective filters and indexes at source boundaries;
- whether a repeated calculation should be materialized;
- whether a BI visual needs a wide convenience table;
- whether a bridge can cause ambiguous filter paths;
- whether an aggregate table can answer a common question more cheaply;
- whether high-cardinality text belongs in a separate detail table.

Denormalization is a tool, not a default. Duplicate a value when there is a
clear consumer or performance reason and document the source of truth.

## 17. Common anti-patterns

- not stating the grain;
- joining several one-to-many tables before aggregating;
- using `DISTINCT` to hide duplicate facts;
- storing lists in comma-separated text;
- physically repeating dimension columns in every fact without a reason;
- allowing facts without a defined date or entity key;
- mixing current-state and historical rows in one model without a status rule;
- applying business filters differently in different marts;
- putting every calculation into Power BI so different reports disagree;
- putting every presentation concern into the warehouse;
- treating a surrogate key as proof that the business grain is unique.

## 18. Modelling review checklist

Before publishing a model, verify:

- Can the grain be stated in one sentence?
- Is the row identity enforced or tested?
- Are all joins compatible with the grain?
- Are many-to-many relationships represented by bridges?
- Are measures additive at the dimensions where users will aggregate them?
- Are active and historical populations explicit?
- Are unknown and missing dimension members handled?
- Are source, intermediate, and mart responsibilities separated?
- Is the model documented for BI users?
- Are key business rules tested?
- Is the Power BI relationship design unambiguous?
